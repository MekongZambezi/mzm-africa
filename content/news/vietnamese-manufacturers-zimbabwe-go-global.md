Many Vietnamese manufacturers have spent two decades winning export orders. For a growing number, the next step is to produce or sell from inside a foreign market. Vietnam's government now supports that step directly, and Zimbabwe is one market worth a serious assessment. This article sets out the business case, the real risks, and a low-cost way to test the opportunity.

## What Go Global offers Vietnamese firms

On 6 April 2026 the Government issued Decision 626/QD-TTg, approving the International Market Expansion Programme for 2026 to 2030, known as Go Global. Its targets to 2030 include training at least 10,000 enterprise participants in global investment and business, advising 1,000 enterprises on international expansion plans, and giving in-depth support to at least 100 enterprises investing abroad, 30 percent of them through mergers and acquisitions.

The programme follows Politburo Resolution 68-NQ/TW of 4 May 2025, which names the private sector the most important driver of the national economy. A new decree on outward investment, Decree 103/2026/ND-CP, took effect on 3 April 2026 and replaced the outward investment chapter of Decree 31/2021/ND-CP.

Outward investment is rising quickly. Vietnam's Statistics Office reports that total outward investment, new and additional capital combined, reached USD 1.36 billion in 2025, up 88.7 percent on 2024. In the first nine months of 2025 the leading destinations were Laos, the Philippines, Indonesia, Germany and the United States. No African country was in the top five.

## Where Vietnam-Zimbabwe trade stands

Two-way trade reached USD 64.6 million in 2025, according to Vietnam's Ministry of Foreign Affairs. Vietnam buys tobacco materials, fruit and vegetables, and cotton. It sells fertiliser, paper and paper products, plastic products, and vehicles and parts. Vietnam runs a trade deficit with Zimbabwe.

Zimbabwe's own figures, as reported to the UN Comtrade database, show much smaller imports from Vietnam: about USD 0.6 million in 2024, against USD 71.7 million of Zimbabwean exports to Vietnam. The most likely explanation is that Vietnamese goods reach Zimbabwe through ports and traders in South Africa and Mozambique and are recorded under those countries. For a manufacturer the point is practical. Vietnamese products are already sold in Zimbabwe, but through intermediaries who keep the margin and the customer relationship.

## What Zimbabwe offers a manufacturer

**Market access.** Zimbabwe is a member of SADC and COMESA. It ratified the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) agreement on 24 May 2019, and it is among the countries that ratified the COMESA-EAC-SADC Tripartite Free Trade Area, which entered into force in July 2024. Under the interim Economic Partnership Agreement with the European Union, applied since May 2012, goods originating in Zimbabwe enter the EU free of duty and quota. The word "originating" matters, as explained below.

**Investment law.** The Zimbabwe Investment and Development Agency (ZIDA) Act of 2019 created a One Stop Investment Services Centre. It guarantees investors against expropriation except in the public interest, and then with compensation, and it allows profits to be sent abroad in freely convertible currency. Investors in special economic zones receive 10-year licences and may move funds for approved activities without exchange control permission.

**Industrial policy.** Zimbabwe's National Development Strategy 2 (NDS2) runs from January 2026 to December 2030 and places industrialisation and value addition at the centre of its priorities.

**The case for local production.** Because no trade preference applies between the two countries, goods shipped from Vietnam pay Zimbabwe's standard import duties. In its agreement with the EU, Zimbabwe also kept duties on about 20 percent of EU imports, including paper, plastics and rubber, textiles and clothing, footwear, glass and ceramics, consumer electronics and vehicles. Several of these are product lines Vietnam already sells to Zimbabwe. A firm that produces inside Zimbabwe avoids import duty on its finished goods and competes against imports that pay it.

**Special economic zones.** ZIDA is promoting the Goromonzi Agro-Industrial Park as a special economic zone, with a first phase of 1,025 hectares about 40 km from Harare. Vietnamese firms already operate under foreign zone regimes: Viglacera has developed the 256-hectare ViMariel industrial park inside Cuba's Mariel Special Development Zone.

## The risks, stated plainly

**Currency.** Zimbabwe runs a multi-currency system that is legal until 31 December 2030. The Zimbabwe Gold (ZiG) was introduced in April 2024 and circulates alongside the US dollar. The Reserve Bank plans a gradual move to the ZiG as the only currency for domestic transactions by 2030. Plan for the possibility that local sales revenue will have to be converted into ZiG.

**Payments.** Before signing any contract, ask your bank how a US dollar payment to or from a Zimbabwean bank would be routed, how long it would take and what it would cost.

**Logistics.** Zimbabwe is landlocked. The nearest port, Beira in Mozambique, is about 560 km from Harare by road. Durban in South Africa is about 1,680 km away. Budget for longer transit times and higher stock levels.

**No treaty protection.** The two countries signed a Trade Agreement and a framework agreement on economic, trade, cultural and scientific cooperation in 2001. There is no free trade agreement, no investment protection agreement and no double taxation agreement. In November 2025 the two governments discussed studying a bilateral trade agreement and an investment protection agreement. Until a tax treaty exists, profits may be taxed in both countries, so tax advice belongs at the start of any investment design.

**Rules of origin.** Preferential access applies only to goods that meet the origin rules of each agreement. Goods made in Vietnam and relabelled in Zimbabwe will not qualify. Real manufacturing or processing must take place in Zimbabwe, and each agreement sets its own test.

## A sensible first step

Before committing capital, answer three questions:

1. Which of your products would meet SADC or COMESA origin rules if finished in Zimbabwe?
2. What is the landed cost and import duty today for the same product shipped from Vietnam?
3. Which Go Global support, such as training or advisory help, can your company apply for?
